Panel-able area

Your lines already imply the roof faces, so they shade in as you draw — no need to trace them. Click any face that could never take a panel: a wall caught inside the outline, a canopy, glass. Click it again to unmark.

Three kinds of line

Seen from above, which is how you’ll be looking at them.
Ridge line
Any fold where the roof falls away on both sides. Flat along the top (a ridge) or running downhill to a corner (a hip) — both go here. They are the same thing to the model, so do not spend time deciding which. A roof shaped angle–flat–angle has several of these; mark them all.
Valley line
Any other fold — the two faces meet at an angle, but the roof does not turn over the top. Two common shapes:
• an internal corner, where two roof sections join, most often on an L or T shaped house;
• a change of pitch, where a steep face runs into a shallow one — say 60° into 20° — and the roof keeps falling the same way, just less steeply.

Ignore where water goes. Plenty of valley lines are nowhere near a gutter and nothing would ever collect in them; the pitch-change case above is the obvious one. What the model needs is that the surface folds here, not that it drains here.
Cliff line
Not a fold at all — the surface stops and the height jumps. Three cases, all the same thing:
• a stepped or split-level roof;
• the sides of something raised — a dormer, clerestory, plant box, lift overrun;
• where the roof itself ends short of the yellow outline — see below.

How to draw it: put the line along the top edge of the higher surface, following the lip where it drops away. For a dormer, that means drawing round its sides — one line per side.
The quick test, in order:
1. Does the surface stop and the height jump? Cliff line.
2. Otherwise it is a fold. Does the roof turn over here — falling away on both sides, whether along the top or running down to a corner? Ridge line.
3. It folds but does not turn over. Valley line.

There are only three because that is all the code actually uses: does the surface break (stops and drops), or fold — and if it folds, does it turn over or not? Ridge versus hip, or which folds a builder would call valleys, is vocabulary the pipeline never consults, so it is not worth your time.

When the roof ends before the yellow outline. The outline is a building footprint, not a roof, and the two often disagree — it can take in a deck, a carport, a ground-level lean-to, or simply be drawn too generously. Where the roof genuinely stops short, that edge is a Cliff line: the surface ends and the height drops to the ground, which is a cliff by any other name. Draw it along the real roof edge, then switch to No panels here and click the strip left outside it.
This is worth the twenty seconds. Left unmarked, the code treats that strip as roof and puts panels on a driveway.

You do not need to trace roof edges that already sit on the yellow outline — those are known. Only the ones that disagree with it.

If you cannot tell whether a line is a fold or a break at all, leave it out rather than guess — a wrong label is worse than a missing one.
