1 Survey Inputs 2 processes · 11 steps

Everything the estimate is allowed to see: the laser survey of the ground and roofs, the aerial photography, and the government register of where buildings are. The district is cut into regions, each building is given exactly one owning region so nothing is counted twice, and the inputs are audited before hours of compute are spent on them.

Rules this part holds to

  • A building belongs to exactly one region -- the region whose box holds its centroid deepest inside, so its survey data is least likely to be clipped at a boundary
  • Only LiDAR returns classified as 'building' are treated as roof, unless there are fewer than 10 of them, in which case all returns are used
  • Missing imagery is accepted (rural New Zealand has none) but must be announced: without it, roof-crease cutting and obstruction detection each lose half their evidence
  • The roof is taken to be the surface the laser sees, not the surveyed wall footprint -- 17.9% of roof area sits outside the outline (0.45 m median eave overhang plus carports and verandas), and this can only ever add area, never remove it
Gather the district's survey data6 steps

Decide which regions make up the district, pull LINZ building outlines, the surface model, aerial imagery and LiDAR tiles for each, give every building exactly one owning region, and check the inputs are real before spending hours of compute on them.

Starts when ./src/run_district_build.sh

  1. Work out which regions the district is

    The region list is the union of the configured regions and what is actually on disk, never a hard-coded list: the config held 23 entries while disk held 24, and two district rebuilds silently skipped the town centre.

    src/region_build.py:56

  2. Ask LINZ where the buildings are

    Pulls the national building-outline layer for the region's bounding box. Outlines are a wall-line footprint surveyed by LINZ, which is why they are trusted for straightness but not for roof extent.

    src/fetch_data.py:31

  3. Order the surface model and the aerial photos

    Requests the 1 m elevation surface and 0.1 m aerial imagery per region, splitting anything over 8 km2 into chunks and mosaicking them back, because imagery runs ~0.35 GB per km2.

    src/fetch_regions.py:53

  4. Pull the raw laser survey

    Downloads the LiDAR tiles that are the primary input. At ~5.8 building-class points per square metre they carry roughly six times the detail of the 1 m gridded surface, and classification lets tree and ground returns be thrown out.

    src/fetch_pointcloud_regions.py

  5. Give each building exactly one region

    Region boxes overlap, so the same building is fetched several times. Each is assigned to the region whose box holds its centroid deepest inside -- the one least likely to have clipped its elevation or imagery at the edge -- which makes the final district merge a plain concatenation.

    src/region_build.py:166

  6. Check the inputs before trusting them

    Ninety seconds of checking before hours of compute. One run built 14 regions with no imagery at all, and built 50 buildings as zeros because their elevation model described ground 340 m west of every building in it. Neither raised an error; both looked like real results.

    src/run_district_build.sh:88
Turn laser returns into a roof surface5 steps

Decide which laser returns are this roof, at what resolution, and how far the roof really extends -- which is further than the surveyed outline says.

Starts when PointCloudSource.points_in_bbox

  1. Keep only the returns that are building

    Uses the survey's own classification to drop ground and vegetation returns. Below 10 building-class points the classification is not trustworthy and all returns are used instead.

    src/pointcloud_source.py:28

  2. Grid the points finer than the 1 m model

    Rasterises the irregular point set onto a finer regular grid (median height per cell), so every downstream stage sees the same shape of data it got from the 1 m surface model but at the survey's real density.

    src/pointcloud_source.py

  3. Model only the top storey

    A multi-level building returns points from every level it exposes. Only the top of the cloud is kept, so a roof is not fitted through a deck two floors below it.

    src/roof_partition.py:946

  4. Find the roof the laser sees, not the walls

    Grows the roof-height surface outward from the outline to recover eaves (0.45 m median overhang) and attached carports and verandas -- 17.9% of roof area. It excludes neighbours and anything not contiguous, and is unioned with the original outline so it can only ever ADD area.

    src/roof_extent.py:75

  5. Take out courtyards and open decks

    Removes parts of the footprint that carry no roof at all, so panels are never proposed over a light well or an open deck.

    src/roof_partition.py:2415

What this hands to the rest

  • Reading the RoofThe roof surface, aerial imagery and building outline are the raw evidence every face is cut from; where imagery is missing, cutting falls back to laser data alone.
  • Sunlight and YieldThe wide bare-earth terrain model and the 1 m surface model are what the far and near horizons are traced through.
2 Reading the Roof 5 processes · 25 steps

Turning one building footprint into the flat faces a panel could actually lie on. Faces are built as a partition cut only by straight lines, because real roofs are straight-edged; complexity is spent only where the laser points say the roof has it. Four sources of face geometry compete, with the owner's own hand-drawn roofs outranking every automatic one, and a roof the pipeline cannot read is shown with a reason instead of a confident-looking guess.

Rules this part holds to

  • Every face boundary is either a surveyed footprint edge or a straight cut -- no boundary is ever traced from a raster image
  • A region of roof is kept whole while one plane explains its points and is cut when it does not, so a simple gable stops after one cut and a hotel keeps going
  • Faces the owner drew are exempt from every automatic quality gate, including the confidence withholding and the plane-fit test -- those tests ask how well points fit planes WE fitted, which is the wrong question about his geometry
  • Where the owner has marked obstructions, only his markup counts; the automatic detector is discarded for that roof entirely
  • Below 45% of roof points lying within 15 cm of the fitted planes, faces are still drawn but no panels are proposed -- a confident layout on a roof we have not understood carries authority it has not earned
  • A roof slope over 55 degrees is not modelled
  • A building that cannot be modelled stays on the map carrying a plain-English reason, never silence
Resolve a roof into flat faces6 steps

Cut the footprint into planar faces using only straight lines, refining until the planes explain the laser points, then check each face is the best explanation of the points underneath it.

Starts when segment_building_best()

  1. Choose where the face geometry comes from

    Four readings compete, in a fixed order of authority: the owner's drawn faces, then the precomputed best-of-SAM-or-lines reading, then vision lines, then the recursive partition. Whichever wins is the roof.

    src/roof_segmentation.py:2500

  2. Take the cut angles from the walls below

    Ridges, hips and valleys run parallel or perpendicular to the walls far more often than not, so the surveyed outline is a better source of cut directions than anything recoverable from a 5.7-point-per-m2 cloud.

    src/roof_partition.py:274

  3. Cut only where one plane is not enough

    A region is kept whole while a single plane explains 85% of its points within 15 cm, and cut when it does not -- then each half is asked the same question. A simple gable stops after one cut; a hotel keeps going. Complexity is spent only where the roof actually has it.

    src/roof_partition.py:714

  4. Re-join faces that are really one surface

    Two pieces that meet at a negligible angle with no height step at their join are one roof plane split by a cut that should not have happened, and are merged back.

    src/roof_partition.py:846

  5. Make each face's angle match its own points

    A face can end up paired with a plane fitted on a different stage's points -- one 62 m2 face claimed 22 degrees while its own points were flat. The plane is refitted from the points inside the face and adopted only on a clear win, and anything over 55 degrees is rejected.

    src/roof_segmentation.py:2335

  6. Score how well the planes explain the roof

    The area-weighted share of roof points lying within 30 cm of the faces we are about to place panels on. This single number decides whether the roof gets a layout at all.

    src/build_layout_geojson.py:71
Turn the owner's drawn lines into roof faces5 steps

Every automatic signal tried failed to separate the roofs the owner calls right from the ones he calls wrong, so he draws the true roof lines himself on plain imagery and those drawings become the roof -- outranking every automatic reading.

Starts when mark_roofs.html (published tool) -> data/roof_labels.json

  1. Show him the roof with nothing on it

    Imagery only, a metre grid for scale, no model output overlaid. Showing the current faces would anchor his answer to what the pipeline already believes, which is the thing under question.

    src/mark_roofs.py:1

  2. Collect ridges, hips, valleys and no-panel areas

    He traces the lines he can see, plus faces he marks as taking no panels and obstructions he outlines himself. These are ground truth for roof STRUCTURE, which nothing else in the project has.

    mark_roofs.html

  3. Seal the drawn lines into closed areas

    A person stops a ridge where it visually stops, so the drawing is nearly, but never exactly, a closed subdivision. Endpoints within tolerance are snapped together, dangling ends are extended along their own direction until they meet something, and only then are faces formed. The sealing is deliberately local -- sealing to the surveyed outline would bake its offset into every face.

    src/label_geometry.py:65

  4. Give each drawn face a slope and aspect

    Each face takes the plane its own points support; a face too small to have enough survey under it deliberately borrows a neighbour's plane rather than being dropped.

    src/roof_partition.py:1687

  5. Exempt his roofs from the automatic gates

    Confidence withholding and the big-roof plane-fit test both ask how well the points fit planes WE fitted, which is upside down on a face he drew. Left unguarded these withheld 892 panels across 23 buildings, five of them roofs he had personally labelled.

    src/build_layout_geojson.py:398
Choose between two independent readings of a roof5 steps

No single automatic method wins everywhere -- line detection is best on crease-textured hip houses, surface segmentation is best where faces differ in tone. Rather than merge them (every attempt degraded the winner), each produces a complete face-set and a scorer picks one per roof.

Starts when data/selected_faces/*.json (precomputed)

  1. Read the roof as tonal surfaces

    A segmentation model splits the imagery into regions that differ in colour or material, producing a complete candidate set of faces. Best where roof faces look different from each other.

    src/face_candidates.py:103

  2. Read the roof as a network of creases

    A line detector fires on folds; fragments that are nearly collinear and nearly touching are merged into creases, then extended into a connected graph. Detected strokes almost never meet (2.6% touching pairs against 24.3% in the owner's markup), and a subdivision needs a graph, so the topology has to be rebuilt before faces can be formed.

    src/line_network.py:1

  3. Score each reading against the evidence

    Each candidate face-set is measured against the imagery and the laser points, and the better one is used for that roof. Roofs where both score poorly belong in the owner's markup queue, not in a guess.

    src/face_candidates.py:1372

  4. Validate the scorer only against his faces

    The scorer's authority is not taken on faith: whether it picks the candidate that agrees better with the owner's own drawn faces is the only accepted validation. A model learned from his markup was measured (0.577 agreement against the shipping chain's 0.714) and deliberately not shipped.

    src/face_regions.py:1

  5. Cover the roof the reading left out

    Whatever the chosen reading did not cover is filled in as residual area, so adopting a reading can never leave part of a roof unmodelled.

    src/roof_partition.py:1339
Flag what a panel must not sit on5 steps

Skylights, vents, air-conditioning plant and existing solar arrays. Height data is blind to anything flush with the roof, so colour is often the only signal -- and being wrong in the cautious direction costs roof area, not a panel mounted over a skylight.

Starts when detect_obstructions_combined()

  1. Find colour that differs from its own neighbourhood

    Each pixel is compared to a blurred version of its immediate surroundings, not to the whole face. Comparing to the whole face meant one large anomaly (a plant yard) inflated the spread enough to hide a small vent elsewhere on the same roof.

    src/obstruction_detection.py:1

  2. Keep only blobs the size of real equipment

    Flagged pixels are grouped and kept only if the group falls in a plausible object-size range, which drops shadow edges and staining noise.

    src/obstruction_detection.py

  3. Over-cover each object rather than trace it exactly

    Detection reliably lights up an object's edge, not its interior, so each blob is hulled and buffered outward. Deliberately errs toward covering too much: the cost is a little roof area, not a panel on a skylight.

    src/obstruction_detection.py

  4. Add the things that stand proud of the plane

    Laser returns that sit well off the face's own plane are added as obstructions, catching the equipment that has height but no colour contrast.

    src/obstruction_detection.py

  5. Let his markup replace the detector entirely

    Where the owner has outlined a roof's obstructions, only his outlines count. On one bright membrane roof the detector hallucinated 140 m2 of blockage against his careful 17 m2. Where he has spoken, only he speaks.

    src/build_layout_geojson.py:455
Say nothing rather than guess4 steps

A roof the pipeline has not understood gets no layout, and a building it cannot model at all stays on the map carrying a plain-English reason -- because a homeowner who finds nothing cannot tell whether we think their roof is hopeless or the site is broken.

Starts when _build_one_at()

  1. Withhold the layout below 45% confidence

    Faces are still drawn so the roof appears on the map, but no panels are proposed. The threshold separates genuinely unmodelled roofs rather than trimming a continuum: the worst sampled roof measures 35%, the next worst 52%.

    src/build_layout_geojson.py:56

  2. Work out WHY a roof produced nothing

    Classifies the failure: too steep to model, not enough laser survey, no usable surface, covered by obstructions, no area large enough for a panel, recorded as demolished, too complex to finish, or modelling failed.

    src/build_layout_geojson.py:256

  3. Keep the building on the map with the reason

    The reason is written in plain English onto the building record, so the site says 'too steep to model' rather than showing an empty roof or nothing at all.

    src/derive_solar_potential.py:28

  4. Drop a roof that would cost the district

    One 16,000 m2 airport-scale roof once ran unbounded and stalled a district build for four and a half hours. A building over its time budget is dropped and reported by id -- never silently.

    src/build_layout_geojson.py:122

What this hands to the rest

3 Placing the Panels 4 processes · 22 steps

Laying real 1.13 x 1.96 m panels on each face the way an installer would rack them: in aligned rows parallel to the roof edge, clear of the eaves, ridges, obstructions and the owner's marked fold lines. The layout is then tidied into believable arrays, post-checked against the laser survey, and finally audited for the things that make a layout LOOK wrong rather than merely be geometrically legal.

Rules this part holds to

  • Panels are packed in the face's own on-surface frame, with the up-slope direction stretched by 1/cos(slope), because a panel lying on a tilted roof covers more roof than its plan-view shadow suggests
  • Two different clearances: 0.3 m from the building's real outer edge, 0.25 m from the face's own boundary -- applied once each, never compounded (compounding cost 5.6% of all roof area and created the 'confetti' look)
  • A shallower fold than 9 degrees keeps only a token 5 cm clearance; a real ridge keeps the full amount
  • A tighter setback is only adopted if it wins at least 2 more panels -- one panel jammed against an edge is scrappiness, not capacity
  • Contiguous clusters under 4 panels are dropped unless they are all the roof has
  • On a roof over 1,000 m2, a face must be a believable plane on its own AND take at least 8 panels, or its panels are demoted to the hidden 'straggler' band rather than deleted -- deleting is a verdict, demotion is a ranking
  • A panel is deleted if the survey beneath it contradicts a roof: under 2 m above local ground, or points that do not fit its own plane. Absence of data is never a veto -- fewer than 8 returns means 'unknown', so the panel is kept
  • A panel keeping under 45% of the year's direct beam is not proposed at all
Pack panels onto one roof face6 steps

Lay real 1.13 x 1.96 m panels in aligned rows the way an installer racks them, in the face's own on-surface frame, clear of the eaves, the ridge, obstructions and drawn fold lines.

Starts when fit_panels_on_facet()

  1. Unroll the tilted face onto a flat plane

    A panel lying on a tilted roof covers more roof than its plan-view shadow suggests, so the up-slope direction is stretched by 1/cos(slope) before anything is packed. Packing against the plan-view shape would mis-count panels.

    src/panel_fitting.py:1

  2. Remove obstructions and drawn fold lines

    Obstructions are subtracted before any clearance is applied. The owner's drawn fold lines become no-panel strips even where the partition did not split on them, and are subtracted raw -- passed as obstructions they picked up ridge clearance a second time and emptied one roof's narrow sawtooth faces.

    src/panel_fitting.py:376

  3. Apply the edge and ridge clearances once each

    0.3 m from the building's real outer edge, 0.25 m from the face's own boundary, whichever is stricter at any point. They used to compound into a 1.10 m gap across every ridge -- 5.6% of all roof area, and most of the 'confetti' look. A fold gentler than 9 degrees keeps only a 5 cm token gap.

    src/panel_fitting.py:390

  4. Rack parallel to the roof edge, not the fitted angle

    Real installers align panels with the eave, and the fitted plane's aspect can be several degrees off the actual eave line. The packing axes come from the face's own minimum bounding rectangle instead.

    src/panel_fitting.py:66

  5. Choose portrait or landscape

    Both are packed and landscape only wins if it fits more than 10% more panels. Below that the tidier conventional orientation is worth more than the extra panel -- and the two halves of one roof then agree with each other.

    src/panel_fitting.py:189

  6. Try a tighter clearance, keep it only for a real gain

    A 3.4 m strip loses a whole row to a 0.3 m setback each side but takes three rows at 0.2 m. The tighter rung is adopted only if it wins at least two more panels -- one panel jammed against an edge is the scrappiness the owner asked us not to produce.

    src/panel_fitting.py:447

Group panels into physical arrays, drop or demote the scraps, and rank every panel so the density slider grows the array the way a staged install actually grows.

    Panels within 35 cm of each other are one physical array. Each panel records its array and that array's size, so the frontend can filter to clean arrays without a three-hour rebuild.

    src/panel_fitting.py:855

  2. Drop clusters too small to be an install

    A contiguous cluster under four panels is dropped -- unless it is the only thing the roof has, because some small roofs genuinely fit only a scrap and that scrap is still worth showing.

    Ranks 1-80 are the main arrays and 81-100 are stragglers, so the default 80% view shows exactly the clean install and 100% shows everything feasible.

Delete panels the survey contradicts5 steps

A post-check over every placed panel: is there really a roof under it, is it flat, and does it get enough sun to be worth proposing. A veto requires evidence AGAINST a roof -- absence of data never deletes a panel.

Starts when python src/gate_panels.py

  1. Require the panel to stand above the ground

    A panel's surface must be at least 2 m above local ground-class laser returns. This catches carparks, yards and buildings demolished since the survey. Ground classification is used rather than the building flag, which is unreliable -- one real commercial roof carried the building flag on only 24% of its returns.

    src/gate_panels.py:43

  2. Never veto on missing data

    Fewer than eight returns beneath a panel means the survey is too thin to judge, so the panel is kept. One real roof at 2.0 points/m2 lost 63 of 69 panels to absolute-count thresholds before this rule existed.

    src/gate_panels.py:56

  3. Require the surface under the panel to be flat

    Points under one 2 m panel must fit their own plane within 0.28 m RMS, and returns more than 0.35 m below the panel's roof level are not the roof. This catches vents, plant and level changes the obstruction pass missed.

    src/gate_panels.py:50

  4. Refuse a panel under a tree or a neighbour

    A panel keeping less than 45% of the year's direct beam is under something and is not proposed at all. Deliberately a near-field test only: the far terrain correction lowers yield but must not delete panels a whole valley deserves.

    src/build_layout_geojson.py:52

  5. Take the best of several candidate layouts (off by default)

    Placement is unstable in its own input: a 10 cm nudge to the outline moves the panel count by a median 26%. Best-of-two is worth +3.45% for double the compute -- measured through the real pipeline, not the +11.4% a simplified harness first suggested -- so it ships switched off.

    src/build_layout_geojson.py:316

The geometric audits pass on layouts the owner calls bad, because he reacts to something else: half-empty roofs, arrays broken into confetti, two racking angles on one roof, obstructions eating a third of the building.

Starts when python src/audit_layout_quality.py

  1. How much of the resolved roof is actually used

    Panel plan area over resolved roof area. The district median was 54% when this was written.

    src/audit_layout_quality.py:86

  2. How many separate islands the panels form

    A real install is one or two compact arrays; twelve islands is confetti. Also counts the lone singles and pairs that read as noise.

4 Sunlight and Yield 5 processes · 20 steps

How much electricity a roof face actually makes. Ideal clear-sky sun is calculated for the latitude, cut back to real local cloudiness using ground-station data, then cut again for the mountains, trees and neighbours that block this particular building's sky. The result is converted to AC kWh with a single stated system loss, and cross-checked against an independent public tool so the whole table cannot be quietly biased.

Rules this part holds to

  • Clear-sky irradiance is scaled by a measured cloud factor; a nearby NIWA ground station is preferred over NASA POWER, whose 1-degree grid averages the whole district and overstates local sun by 8-22%
  • Direct beam is zeroed for any hour the sun sits below the skyline in that direction; diffuse skylight is left untouched -- a stated simplification shared with PVGIS
  • Far terrain (8 m DEM) and near clutter (1 m surface model within 300 m) are combined by taking the higher skyline per direction, so the same blocker is never counted twice
  • The building's own footprint is excluded from its own near horizon
  • Shading is computed per face and per panel, not per building -- a single building-centroid value lands on the largest open face and hides the shading on its wings
  • The far-terrain correction lowers yield but must never delete a panel; only the near-field deep-shade test can do that
  • Total system loss is 14% INCLUDING the inverter, applied multiplicatively (0.97 x 0.8866 = 0.860); cell temperature is rolled into that flat figure rather than modelled separately
  • Seasonal curves deliberately exclude terrain, because the browser applies each building's own terrain mask on top -- including it made winter afternoons ~5x too low
Work out how much sun a slope and aspect get5 steps

Ideal clear-sky sun for the latitude, cut back to real local cloudiness and to the mountains that block the sky, then transposed onto every combination of roof tilt and direction and cached.

Starts when SolarModel / build_poa_lookup_table()

  1. Compute an ideal clear-sky year

    Hourly direct and diffuse irradiance for the location over a representative year, from a standard clear-sky model. This is the upper bound everything else cuts down.

    src/solar_model.py:360

  2. Cut it back to real local cloudiness

    A measured monthly ratio of real to clear-sky sun. A nearby NIWA ground station is preferred; NASA POWER is the portable fallback and is explicitly distrusted -- its 1-degree grid averages the whole district and overstates local sun by 8-22%, worst in winter.

    src/solar_model.py:89

  3. Take away the sun the mountains block

    Rays are marched outward through the nationwide 8 m terrain model to find the highest skyline angle in each compass direction -- averaging about 9 degrees and peaking near 17 around Queenstown. Direct beam is zeroed for any hour the sun sits below it; diffuse skylight is left untouched, a stated simplification shared with PVGIS.

    src/terrain_horizon.py:53

  4. Tilt the sky onto the roof plane

    The corrected horizontal irradiance is transposed onto each tilt and azimuth and integrated over the year, giving kWh per square metre a face of that shape actually receives.

    src/solar_model.py:360

  5. Cache it in 5-degree slope by 10-degree aspect bins

    A few hundred model runs cover thousands of faces, because only around 350 distinct slope/aspect bins exist in a district. Every face's irradiance is a lookup, not a recomputation.

    src/solar_model.py:477
Take away this building's own sky5 steps

The shared district horizon cannot see the hill behind one street or the tree next door. Every building gets its own 72-direction skyline, built from far terrain and near clutter combined so no blocker is counted twice.

Starts when bake_building_horizons / building_shading_factor

  1. Stand at the roof's low edge

    The observer is the footprint centroid at eave height -- the 15th percentile of surface height inside the footprint. One ray origin under-represents variation across a very large roof, which is a stated caveat.

    src/building_horizon.py:52

  2. Scan the distant ranges

    The wide bare-earth 8 m model out to its full extent, so a valley-floor building gets a genuinely different sky from a hilltop one three streets away. Replaces the single shared per-area profile.

    src/building_horizon.py:179

  3. Scan the trees and neighbours within 300 m

    The 1 m surface model out to 300 m, with the building's own footprint excluded unconditionally. Tree heights are as of the survey vintage, which is a stated caveat.

    src/building_horizon.py:75

  4. Combine them by taking the higher skyline

    Per direction, the maximum of the far and near profiles. The far/near split is precisely what stops the same blocker being subtracted twice.

    src/building_horizon.py:121

  5. Apply it per face and per panel, not per building

    A single building-centroid value lands on the largest, most open face and hides real shading on the building's wings -- measured 0.50-1.00 across one building's twelve faces where the centroid version gave 0.998 for all of them.

    src/building_shading.py:76
Turn irradiance into kilowatt-hours4 steps

The one conversion from sun on the panel to AC electricity out of the inverter, with a single stated loss figure rather than a stack of hidden ones.

Starts when SolarModel.facet_yield()

  1. Convert irradiance to full-power hours

    Because a panel's rating is defined at 1,000 W/m2, kWh of plane-of-array irradiance per m2 converts directly to equivalent full-rated-power hours. A 500 W panel of 2.22 m2, 22.5% efficient, is the modelled unit.

    src/solar_model.py:519

  2. Apply 14% total system loss, compounded

    Inverter 97% multiplied by the rest to reach exactly 14% total -- so the written derate is 11.34%, not a round number, because losses compound rather than add. 5 Cost, Savings and Payback 3 processes · 15 steps

Turning kilowatt-hours into dollars: what the system costs at this size, whether the building is billed as a home or a business, how much of the generation is used on site versus exported, and how long the system takes to pay for itself. An hourly engine prices each hour of a representative day per season so time-of-use plans and batteries mean something, instead of being faked as a bigger daytime load.

Rules this part holds to

  • Install cost is tiered by system size, from $3,000/kW under 3 kW down to $1,200/kW over 50 kW
  • Home or business is decided by the district plan's zoning, from the council. Roof area and system size are only a backstop where a zone genuinely holds both uses -- roof area alone mislabelled 205 of 1,066 pilot buildings, in both directions
  • Self-consumption is a LOAD, not a share of output: the site can absorb at most its daytime kW for 6.7 sun hours a day, and never more than it uses in a year. So the self-consumed kWh stays flat as the array grows and the SHARE falls
  • Daytime load scales with annual consumption rather than sitting beside it, so raising a household's yearly use actually raises what it can self-consume
  • Retail prices inflate at 4%/yr; the export rate declines at 2%/yr from today's rate and never levels off; everything is discounted back at 3%
  • Panel output degrades 0.5%/yr, the inverter is replaced at year 15 for 18% of system cost, and a battery is replaced at year 15 -- omitting either replacement flatters the result
  • The marginal panel earns the EXPORT rate, not the retail rate: self-consumption is already met by the panels that came before it
  • The battery charges only from surplus generation, never from the grid, and keeps a 10% reserve floor at 90% round-trip efficiency

Work out what the electricity is worth, year by year6 steps

Split each year's generation into what the site uses itself (worth the retail price) and what it exports (worth the declining buyback rate), then run that out over thirty years with degradation, inflation, discounting and an inverter replacement.

Starts when economicsFor()

  1. Treat self-consumption as a load, not a share

    A house drawing 1.5 kW through the day soaks up the same ~10 kWh whether the array is 5 kW or 40 kW. So the self-consumed kWh is flat and the SHARE falls as the system grows. The old fixed-percentage model did the opposite, which is why big roofs on small houses looked implausibly good.

    economics.js:178

  2. Scale the daytime load with annual consumption

    A house that uses more electricity uses more of it in daylight too. The profile's kW is read as the load at the default annual use and moves proportionally -- before this, editing a household's annual use from 7,000 to 10,000 kWh changed nothing at all, because the kW ceiling always bound first.

    economics.js:186

  3. Cap it by both the daytime load and the annual use

    The site can absorb at most its daytime kW for 6.7 sun hours a day, and can never self-consume more than it uses in a year. Whichever binds first, binds; everything past it is exported. Without the annual cap, oversized systems on small buildings show savings nobody could realise.

    economics.js:200

  4. Inflate retail, decline export, discount everything

    Retail rises 4%/yr, the export rate falls 2%/yr from today's rate and never levels off, generation degrades 0.5%/yr, and every future dollar is discounted back at 3%. The discount rate is on screen and adjustable, so the choice is visible rather than buried.

    economics.js:76

  5. Subtract the inverter replacement

    An inverter does not last the life of the panels. It is replaced at year 15 for 18% of system cost, discounted like any future cost. Leaving it out flatters every system.

    economics.js:221

  6. Find the year the savings cover the cost

    Cumulative discounted value is walked forward until it passes the install cost, interpolating within the crossing year. Separately, the lifetime cents earned by one extra kWh/yr is computed at the EXPORT rate -- the marginal panel's world, because self-consumption is already met by the panels before it.

    economics.js:228
Price each hour instead of each year5 steps

A time-of-use plan only differs from another if it prices electricity differently at different times, and a battery is nothing but a time-shifting device. Neither exists at annual resolution -- the old model faked a battery as a bigger daytime load, crediting it for energy it never stored.

Starts when economicsHourlyFor()

  1. Use four representative days, not 8,760 hours

    One day per season, weighted by the days in it. Two reasons: the generation side is exactly the seasonal curves the chart already draws, so money and chart cannot disagree; and a true 8,760-hour run would need a real half-hourly demand trace, which does not exist here -- faking it would be precision theatre.

    economics.js:268

  2. Give the site a shape of demand through the day

    The household shape is the familiar double peak with a midday trough -- which is exactly why self-consumption is limited and why a battery has something to do. The business shape is flat through the working day. Shapes only; the annual use figure sets the scale.

    economics.js:288

  3. Price the hours under different plan structures

    Flat, day/night, peak/off-peak, high-buyback and a free-hour plan, plus two business plans. These are illustrative shapes of real New Zealand plan types, not offers from any retailer, and are all editable -- which is why none carries a company name.

    economics.js:317

  4. Simulate the battery hour by hour

    It charges only from surplus generation, never from the grid, discharges only into a deficit, respects a power limit, a 10% reserve floor and 90% round-trip efficiency, and is replaced at 15 years -- leaving that replacement out is the easiest way to make a battery look good.

    As generation degrades the battery fills less often, which a single scaling factor could not express -- so each of the thirty years is simulated in full.

6 Publishing the Map 3 processes · 16 steps

Getting the answer in front of the public. A building's headline numbers are derived by adding up its own facets and panels -- so the two map layers cannot contradict each other -- then addresses, confidence and density steps are patched on, regions are merged, and everything is cut into map tiles the browser fetches only where it is looking. A set of guards exists to stop a build that quietly ignored its own inputs from shipping.

Rules this part holds to

  • The building summary IS the sum of its panel layout, derived by aggregation rather than recomputed -- the two layers previously disagreed (135 vs 203 panels on one building)
  • Regions are merged by plain concatenation, because each building was already assigned to exactly one region before the builds ran
  • Ten cumulative density steps (10%..100%) are baked onto every building so the dashboard can total what is in view at any zoom -- a tiled source cannot be summed from what happens to be rendered
  • The whole-file download does not scale: 26 MB for Queenstown's 15,353 buildings would be 3.4 GB for New Zealand's 2.1 million, so per-building detail is split out and fetched on click
  • The previous build is snapshotted before being overwritten, or 'did this help?' becomes unanswerable
  • A build that produces fewer than 50 facets tagged as coming from the selected-faces chain when that chain is enabled FAILS -- a green build that ignored its inputs must not deploy quietly

Refuse to ship a build that ignored its inputs5 steps

The failures that cost this project most were never crashes -- they were plausible-looking numbers nobody could see were wrong. One resumed run skipped every layout stage on stale markers and shipped the previous geometry with fresh timestamps: zero errors, bit-identical totals.

Starts when src/run_district_build.sh

  1. Snapshot the build about to be replaced

    The merge overwrites the district file, and once it is gone every 'did this help?' question becomes unanswerable. Deliberately non-fatal: losing eight hours of compute because the snapshot tripped would be worse than a missing baseline.

    src/run_district_build.sh:64

  2. Check each stage's inputs before it runs

    A stage refuses to run against inputs that are missing or older than they should be -- including refusing to write into a merged file that is already stale.

  3. Remember what has already finished

    Every stage records a completion marker and skips work whose marker is newer than all of its inputs. Before this, one district rebuild was launched three times and each launch redid every completed region.
